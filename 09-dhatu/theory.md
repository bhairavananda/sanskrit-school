# Stage 7: Dhātu — Roots and Upasargas

## Goal
Understand verbal roots (dhātus) as the atomic units of Sanskrit meaning, and learn how upasargas (prefixes) transform them systematically. See the semantic architecture hidden inside sacred vocabulary.

## What is a Dhātu?

A **dhātu** (धातु) is a verbal root — the irreducible seed from which verbs, nouns, adjectives, and entire families of words grow. Pāṇini's Dhātupāṭha lists ~2000 roots. Mastering even 50 core roots unlocks thousands of words.

## The Mantra of Meanings: √grah as Example

**√grah** (to seize/grasp):

| Prefix + Root | Word | Meaning | Shift |
|--------------|------|---------|-------|
| grah | graha | planet (that seizes) | basic |
| anu + grah | **anugraha** | grace, favor | "seize after" → bless |
| ni + grah | **nigraha** | restraint, control | "seize down" → subdue |
| sam + grah | **saṃgraha** | collection | "seize together" → gather |
| pari + grah | **parigraha** | acceptance, embrace | "seize around" → surround |
| vi + grah | **vigraha** | conflict, form | "seize apart" → separate |
| upa + grah | **upagraha** | satellite, support | "seize near" → assist |
| pra + grah | **pragraha** | rein, control | "seize forward" → guide |

From one root, an entire philosophical vocabulary blooms.

## Pratyaya — The Conversion Kit System

A **pratyaya** (प्रत्यय) is a suffix — in LEGO terms, a **conversion kit** that transforms a dhātu (engine) or prātipadika (subassembly) into a different kind of word. There are four major families:

| Pratyaya Family | Attaches to | Creates | Examples |
|-----------------|-------------|---------|----------|
| **Kṛt** | Dhātu (verbal root) | Nouns, adjectives, participles from roots | √kṛ + tṛ → kartṛ (doer), √kṛ + ta → kṛta (done) |
| **Taddhita** | Prātipadika (nominal stem) | Derived nouns/adjectives from existing stems | rājan + ya → rājya (kingdom), guru + tva → gurutva (gravity) |
| **Sup** | Prātipadika (nominal stem) | Inflected nouns (already learned in Stage 5) | deva + -aḥ → devaḥ |
| **Tiṅ** | Dhātu (verbal root) | Inflected verbs (already learned in Stage 6) | √gam + -ti → gacchati |

You have already been using sup (noun connectors) and tiṅ (verb control panels) since earlier stages. Now you see that kṛt and taddhita are two more conversion kit types — they derive NEW stems rather than inflecting existing ones.

### Kṛt-pratyayas (Root-to-Stem Conversion Kits)

These conversion kits attach to dhātus to create new prātipadikas (nominal stems):

| Suffix | Creates | Example |
|--------|---------|---------|
| -ta / -na | past participle | kṛta (done), datta (given) |
| -ti | abstract noun (F) | bhakti, śakti, mukti |
| -ana | action noun (N) | darśana, smaraṇa |
| -tṛ | agent noun | kartā (doer), dātā (giver) |
| -ya | gerundive ("to be X") | pūjya (to be worshipped) |
| -in | possessor | yogin (one who has yoga) |

### How ONE Dhātu Generates a Word Family

Take **√gam** (to go) and watch the system at work:

| Component(s) | Result | Type |
|-------------|--------|------|
| √gam + tiṅ | **gacchati** (goes) | Verb (tiṅ = verb control panel) |
| √gam + ta (kṛt) | **gata** (gone) | Past participle (kṛt = conversion kit) |
| √gam + ana (kṛt) | **gamana** (going) | Action noun |
| √gam + tṛ (kṛt) | **gantṛ** (goer) | Agent noun |
| ā + √gam + tiṅ | **āgacchati** (comes) | Verb with upasarga (modifier attachment) |
| upa + √gam + tiṅ | **upagacchati** (approaches) | Verb with upasarga |
| sam + √gam + a (kṛt) | **saṅgama** (confluence) | Noun with upasarga + kṛt |

One engine (dhātu), combined with modifier attachments (upasargas) and conversion kits (pratyayas), generates an entire vocabulary.

### 🧱 LEGO Callback

**Dhātu (engine) + Upasarga (modifier attachment) + Pratyaya (conversion kit) = the word-generation system.** With these three, you can derive most of the Sanskrit vocabulary.

| Component | LEGO Role | Examples |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| Dhātu (basic brick / engine) | The irreducible meaning-unit that powers everything | √kṛ (do), √bhū (be), √gam (go), √dā (give), √nam (bow), √bhaj (serve) |
| Upasarga (modifier attachment) | Redirects the engine's meaning | pra- (forth), ā- (toward), sam- (together), vi- (apart), anu- (after), upa- (near) |

Every verb you have used since Stage 1 is a dhātu with a tiṅ control panel attached. Every noun stem is a prātipadika — often a dhātu that has been run through a kṛt conversion kit.

## Consonant-Stem Baseplates: From Root to Noun

Many of the most important Sanskrit nouns — especially divine epithets and philosophical terms — end in consonants rather than vowels. Words like ātman (the Self), yogin (the practitioner), bhagavat (the Blessed One), and brahman (the Absolute) are everywhere in devotional and philosophical Sanskrit. You cannot read a single page of the Gītā or Upaniṣads without meeting them.

These stems often derive directly from dhātus — the very roots you have just studied:

| Dhātu | Suffix | Consonant-Stem Noun | Meaning |
|-------|--------|---------------------|---------|
| √yuj (to join) | -in | yogin | one who has yoga |
| √bhaj (to share) | -vat | bhagavat | one who possesses bhaga |
| √bṛh (to expand) | -man | brahman | the vast, the Absolute |
| √rāj (to rule) | -an | rājan | king, ruler |
| √man (to think) | -as/-man | manas / ātman | mind / Self |

"Consonant stem" is NOT one pattern — it is a **family of families**, each with its own declensional behavior. Just as vowel stems (-a, -ā, -i, -u, etc.) each have their own paradigm, so do consonant stems. We learn four sub-families here, each anchored by a deity or sacred model word.

### 1. -an Stems: Ātman (the Self)

**Model words**: ātman (Self), rājan (king), nāman (name)

The -an stems are among the oldest in Sanskrit. Their defining feature is **three-grade stem alternation**:

| Grade | Stem Form | Where Used |
|-------|-----------|------------|
| Strong | ātmān- | Nom sg, Acc sg, Nom/Acc/Voc du |
| Middle | ātman- | Before consonant endings (Inst sg, Dat sg, etc.) |
| Weak | ātma- | Before vowel endings (Inst pl, etc.) and in Loc sg |

**Sacred context**: ātman is the Self — the most important noun in Vedānta. "ayam ātmā brahma" (this Self is Brahman) — Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad. Every time you decline this word, you are handling the central concept of Indian philosophy.

**Full paradigm — ātman (masculine)**:

| Vibhakti | Ekavacana (sg) | Dvivacana (du) | Bahuvacana (pl) |
|----------|---------------|----------------|-----------------|
| Prathamā (nom) | ātmā | ātmānau | ātmānaḥ |
| Dvitīyā (acc) | ātmānam | ātmānau | ātmanaḥ |
| Tṛtīyā (inst) | ātmanā | ātmabhyām | ātmabhiḥ |
| Caturthī (dat) | ātmane | ātmabhyām | ātmabhyaḥ |
| Pañcamī (abl) | ātmanaḥ | ātmabhyām | ātmabhyaḥ |
| Ṣaṣṭhī (gen) | ātmanaḥ | ātmanoḥ | ātmanām |
| Saptamī (loc) | ātmani | ātmanoḥ | ātmasu |
| Sambodhana (voc) | ātman | ātmānau | ātmānaḥ |

Note: The -n drops before consonant-initial case endings in the bha-cases (inst/dat/abl/loc plural). The strong stem (ātmān-) appears in nominative and accusative singular and nominative/accusative/vocative dual.

### 2. -in Stems: Yogin (the Practitioner)

**Model words**: yogin (practitioner of yoga), svāmin (lord/master), pakṣin (bird, "one with wings")

The -in stems are **agent/possessor nouns** — "one who does X" or "one who possesses X." They are formed by adding -in to a noun or root: yoga + in = yogin, svāma + in = svāmin, dharma + in = dharmin.

**Stem alternation**:

| Context | Form | Example |
|---------|------|---------|
| Nom sg | yogī (lengthened) | yogī dhyāyati (the yogin meditates) |
| Acc sg | yoginam | yoginam paśyāmi (I see the yogin) |
| Inst sg onward | yogin- | yoginā saha (with the yogin) |

**Sacred context**: svāmī, yogī, dharmī, kāmī, jñānī — all -in stems. When you hear "-ī" at the end of a masculine noun describing a person's quality or practice, you are almost certainly hearing an -in stem in its nominative singular.

**Full paradigm — yogin (masculine)**:

| Vibhakti | Ekavacana (sg) | Dvivacana (du) | Bahuvacana (pl) |
|----------|---------------|----------------|-----------------|
| Prathamā (nom) | yogī | yoginau | yoginaḥ |
| Dvitīyā (acc) | yoginam | yoginau | yoginaḥ |
| Tṛtīyā (inst) | yoginā | yogibhyām | yogibhiḥ |
| Caturthī (dat) | yogine | yogibhyām | yogibhyaḥ |
| Pañcamī (abl) | yoginaḥ | yogibhyām | yogibhyaḥ |
| Ṣaṣṭhī (gen) | yoginaḥ | yoginoḥ | yoginām |
| Saptamī (loc) | yogini | yoginoḥ | yogiṣu |
| Sambodhana (voc) | yogin | yoginau | yoginaḥ |

Note: The nominative singular is the only form where the stem-final -n is replaced — it becomes yogī with a long -ī. Everywhere else, the -n is present. The -in stems are remarkably regular once you know this one exception.

### 3. -ant/-at Stems: Bhagavat (the Blessed One)

**Model words**: bhagavat (the Blessed One), mahat (great), dhīmat (wise)

These stems are often participial in origin — "the one who possesses" or "the one who is doing." The -vat suffix means "possessing": bhaga (fortune) + vat = bhagavat (one possessing fortune). The -mat suffix is its variant after certain sounds: dhī (intellect) + mat = dhīmat (possessing intellect).

**Stem alternation**:

| Context | Form | Example |
|---------|------|---------|
| Nom sg (strong) | bhagavān | bhagavān uvāca (the Blessed One spoke) |
| Acc sg (strong) | bhagavantam | bhagavantam namāmi (I bow to the Blessed One) |
| Inst sg (weak) | bhagavatā | bhagavatā uktam (spoken by the Blessed One) |

The strong stem ends in -ant (bhagavant-), and the weak stem ends in -at (bhagavat-). In the nominative singular, the -ant becomes -ān with visarga: bhagavān.

**Sacred context**: bhagavān = "the one who possesses bhaga (fortune, power, glory)" — it is a title, not a proper name. The Bhagavad Gītā repeatedly uses "śrī bhagavān uvāca" — and now you know exactly why it is bhagavān in the nominative but bhagavantam in the accusative and bhagavataḥ in the genitive.

**Full paradigm — bhagavat (masculine)**:

| Vibhakti | Ekavacana (sg) | Dvivacana (du) | Bahuvacana (pl) |
|----------|---------------|----------------|-----------------|
| Prathamā (nom) | bhagavān | bhagavantau | bhagavantaḥ |
| Dvitīyā (acc) | bhagavantam | bhagavantau | bhagavataḥ |
| Tṛtīyā (inst) | bhagavatā | bhagavadbhyām | bhagavadbhiḥ |
| Caturthī (dat) | bhagavate | bhagavadbhyām | bhagavadbhyaḥ |
| Pañcamī (abl) | bhagavataḥ | bhagavadbhyām | bhagavadbhyaḥ |
| Ṣaṣṭhī (gen) | bhagavataḥ | bhagavatoḥ | bhagavatām |
| Saptamī (loc) | bhagavati | bhagavatoḥ | bhagavatsu |
| Sambodhana (voc) | bhagavan | bhagavantau | bhagavantaḥ |

Note: The strong stem (bhagavant-) appears in nominative and accusative singular and dual. Everywhere else uses the weak stem (bhagavat-). Before consonant endings, -t may voice to -d (bhagavadbhyām).

### 4. -man Stems: Brahman (the Absolute)

**Model words**: brahman (the Absolute), karman (action), nāman (name)

The -man stems are mostly **neuter** and represent results, instruments, or abstract concepts. They are closely related to the -an stems above but differ critically in the neuter nominative/accusative forms.

Note that brahman as neuter means "the Absolute Reality" (the philosophical principle), while brahmā (masculine -an stem, nominative of brahman used as masculine) refers to the creator deity. Context determines which is meant.

**Sacred context**: brahman (the Absolute), karman (action/deed), dharman (that which upholds — the archaic form from which dharma derives). These are among the most philosophically loaded nouns in all of Sanskrit.

**Full paradigm — brahman (neuter)**:

| Vibhakti | Ekavacana (sg) | Dvivacana (du) | Bahuvacana (pl) |
|----------|---------------|----------------|-----------------|
| Prathamā (nom) | brahma | brahmaṇī | brahmāṇi |
| Dvitīyā (acc) | brahma | brahmaṇī | brahmāṇi |
| Tṛtīyā (inst) | brahmaṇā | brahmabhyām | brahmabhiḥ |
| Caturthī (dat) | brahmaṇe | brahmabhyām | brahmabhyaḥ |
| Pañcamī (abl) | brahmaṇaḥ | brahmabhyām | brahmabhyaḥ |
| Ṣaṣṭhī (gen) | brahmaṇaḥ | brahmaṇoḥ | brahmaṇām |
| Saptamī (loc) | brahmaṇi | brahmaṇoḥ | brahmasu |
| Sambodhana (voc) | brahma / brahman | brahmaṇī | brahmāṇi |

Note: The key neuter difference — nominative and accusative are identical (as with all neuters), and in the singular they show the bare stem without the -n: brahma, not *brahmā. The -n reappears before vowel-initial endings as -ṇ (brahmaṇā, brahmaṇe) due to the retroflexion rule (ṇatva) triggered by the preceding r in brahman.

### Eleven Baseplates

With these four consonant-stem families plus the seven vowel baseplates from Stage 5, you can now decline the vast majority of nouns in devotional, philosophical, and ritual Sanskrit. The full inventory:

| # | Type | Model | Gender | Stage |
|---|------|-------|--------|-------|
| 1 | -a | deva | M | 5 |
| 2 | -a | phala | N | 5 |
| 3 | -ā | devī/senā | F | 5 |
| 4 | -i | agni / mati | M / F | 5 |
| 5 | -u | guru / dhenu | M / F | 5 |
| 6 | -ī | nadī | F | 5 |
| 7 | -ū | vadhū | F | 5 |
| 8 | -an | ātman | M | 9 |
| 9 | -in | yogin | M | 9 |
| 10 | -ant/-at | bhagavat | M | 9 |
| 11 | -man | brahman | N | 9 |

Your baseplate collection has expanded from 7 to 11. The consonant-stem prātipadikas snap onto the same sup connector system — the principle is identical, only the connector shapes change. Where vowel-stem prātipadikas simply receive sup endings after a vowel, consonant-stem prātipadikas must negotiate the junction (sandhi — the physics of the snap) between a final consonant and the sup connector. This negotiation produces the stem alternations (strong/middle/weak) that make consonant stems look harder than they are. Once you see the pattern, the construction logic is the same.
