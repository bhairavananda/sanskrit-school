# Sanskrit School

When you chant **namaḥ**, you are hearing a word built from smaller parts.

When you say **maheśvara**, you are hearing **mahā + īśvara** fused by sandhi.

When you chant **saccidānanda**, three ideas have been compressed into one flowing expression: **sat + cit + ānanda**.

Sanskrit can feel intimidating when you encounter the finished words. But the words are not arbitrary.

They are **built**.

Roots carry meaning. Prefixes reshape it. Suffixes create new words. Case endings tell you what a noun is doing. Verb endings tell you who is acting and how. Sandhi determines what happens when all these pieces meet.

This course teaches you to stop seeing Sanskrit as a wall of vocabulary and start seeing the **system underneath it**.

---

## The Big Idea: Sanskrit Is LEGO

Don't begin by memorizing hundreds of sentences.

Sanskrit is not primarily a phrasebook. It is a **construction system**.

Once you recognize the components and understand how they connect, you can take words apart, see how they were formed, and build new ones yourself.

The goal is simple:

**Stop memorizing Sanskrit. Start generating Sanskrit.**

---

## The Building Block Taxonomy

A LEGO set is not just "bricks." There are bricks, connectors, baseplates, axles, decorative pieces — each with a distinct function. Sanskrit grammar works the same way. Calling everything a "piece" obscures the actual architecture.

Here is the precise inventory:

| Component | Sanskrit Term | LEGO Analogy | What it does |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|-------------|
| **Dhātu** | root | Basic brick / engine | Carries core action or semantic seed. √nam = bow, √gam = go, √kṛ = do |
| **Upasarga** | prefix | Modifier attachment | Attaches before a root, changes/redirects/intensifies meaning |
| **Pratyaya** | suffix | Conversion kit | Transforms a root or stem into another grammatical structure (kṛt, taddhita, sup, tiṅ) |
| **Prātipadika** | nominal stem | Finished subassembly | A stem ready to receive case endings — rāma-, devī-, viṣṇu- |
| **Sup** | case ending | Noun connector | Connects nominal stems to the sentence (case, number, role) |
| **Tiṅ** | verb ending | Verb control panel | Configures finite verbs (person, number, tense/mood) |
| **Sandhi** | junction | Physics of the snap | NOT a brick — describes what happens to sounds when components meet |

This distinction matters. Morphology asks: **what pieces are being attached?** Sandhi asks: **what happens at the joint?** They are different questions, and the course treats them separately.

## The Dhātu as Word-Family Generator

A single root is not just one word. It is a **generator** — a semantic engine that produces an entire family of related forms.

```text
√gam
  gacchati       he goes          (finite verb)
  gata           gone             (past participle)
  gamana         going            (action noun)
  gantṛ          goer             (agent noun)
  āgacchati      he comes         (prefix ā + root)
  upagacchati    he approaches    (prefix upa + root)
```

One root. One meaning-seed. Six different words, each built by attaching different components to the same engine.

This is what the course trains you to see.

---

## How Construction Works

Every Sanskrit word is assembled through a predictable flow. There are two pipelines — one for nominals, one for verbs.

**Nominal construction:**
```text
DHĀTU / BASE → DERIVATION (kṛt / taddhita) → PRĀTIPADIKA (stem) → SUP ENDING → INFLECTED WORD → SANDHI → surface form
```

Example: √nam → namas (kṛt suffix) → namaḥ (sup ending) → surface form.

**Verbal construction:**
```text
UPASARGA + DHĀTU + VERBAL FORMATION + TIṄ → FINITE VERB → SANDHI
```

Example: upa + √viś + verbal formation + ati → upaviśati → "he/she sits down."

### Watch the Pieces Stack

```text
upa + √viś + ati
        ↓
    upaviśati
    "he/she sits down"

pra + √nam
      ↓
   praṇāmaḥ
   "prostration"

rāma + āya
      ↓
    rāmāya
    "for Rāma"

karuṇā + maya + ī
         ↓
    karuṇāmayī
    "she who is full of compassion"
```

Once your eyes learn to recognize these components, unfamiliar Sanskrit becomes much less mysterious.

Instead of seeing:

**karuṇāmayī**

you begin seeing:

**karuṇā + maya + ī**

And eventually you can build the form yourself.

## Sandhi: The Physics of the Snap

Sandhi is **not a building block**. It is what happens at every joint when building blocks meet.

When two LEGO bricks snap together, the connection point has its own physics — the studs deform slightly, the plastic grips. Sanskrit works the same way. The components are one thing. What happens at their seams is another.

```text
śiva + āya
→ śivāya        (vowel + vowel)

mahā + īśvara
→ maheśvara      (ā + ī → e)

sat + cit
→ saccit          (t + c → cc)
```

You have probably been producing sandhi for years simply by chanting mantras and stotras.

The difference is that now you will learn to **see the mechanism**.

Once the major sandhi patterns become automatic, long Sanskrit words stop looking like indivisible objects. You can pull them apart and discover what is hiding inside.

## The Baseplates: Why Rāma and Viṣṇu Behave Differently

Every noun has a **prātipadika** (nominal stem). The final shape of that stem determines which family of sup endings and sound changes it follows.

Think of the stem as the baseplate onto which sup endings are attached. These are properly called **nominal stem types** or **stem endings**, and they determine **declension patterns**. Different prātipadikas have different connector shapes. That is why **Rāma**, **Hari**, **Viṣṇu**, and **Lakṣmī** take different forms even when performing the same grammatical role.

> Do not assume that every word ending in the same visible letter declines identically. Stem type is a grammatical category, not just a spelling pattern.

Instead of memorizing hundreds of unrelated tables, we learn a representative deity or word for each major pattern. The baseplates are introduced in three tiers across the course.

### Core Baseplates (Stage 5)

The seven vowel-stem families every beginner must learn:

| Pattern | Stem | Model | Gender note | Forms |
|---------|------|-------|-------------|-------|
| **-a** | short a | **Rāma** | masc; neuter -a stems (phala, jñāna) differ slightly | rāmaḥ, rāmam, rāmeṇa… |
| **-ā** | long ā | **Durgā** | feminine | durgā, durgām, durgayā… |
| **-i** | short i | **Hari** | masc; fem -i (mati) and neut -i (vāri) also exist | hariḥ, harim, hariṇā… |
| **-ī** | long ī | **Lakṣmī** | feminine | lakṣmīḥ, lakṣmīm, lakṣmyā… |
| **-u** | short u | **Viṣṇu** | masc; fem -u (dhenu) and neut -u (madhu) also exist | viṣṇuḥ, viṣṇum, viṣṇunā… |
| **-ū** | long ū | **Vadhū** | feminine | vadhūḥ, vadhūm, vadhvā… |
| **-ṛ** | vocalic ṛ | **Mātṛ / Pitṛ** | both genders | mātā, mātaram, mātrā… |

### Intermediate Baseplates (Stage 9)

Consonant-stem families that appear constantly in devotional and philosophical Sanskrit:

| Pattern | Stem family | Model | What it covers |
|---------|------------|-------|---------------|
| **-an** | n-stems | **Ātman** | Self, spirit, soul words |
| **-man** | n-stems | **Brahman** | Abstract neuter concepts |
| **-in** | n-stems | **Yogin** | Agent nouns: one who does X |
| **-ant/-at** | participial | **Bhagavat** | "The one who possesses…" — participles and epithets |

> "Consonant stem" is a broad category. Do NOT present -at as simply "consonant." Each sub-family (-an, -in, -ant, -man) has its own declensional behavior.

### Advanced Baseplates (Stage 12+)

For students who already understand the general sup machinery:

| Pattern | Model | What it covers |
|---------|-------|---------------|
| **-as** | manas | Neuter s-stems (thought, mind, action words) |
| **-is** | havis | Neuter s-stems (offering, ritual words) |
| **-us** | cakṣus | Neuter s-stems (sense-organ words) |
| Root stems | vāc, diś, marut | Monosyllabic consonant roots |
| Pronouns | tad, idam, kim | Irregular but essential |
| Numerals | — | Number words with special declension |

### The Pedagogical Principle

> Learn a memorable model for each declension family. Then use that model to generate unfamiliar forms. A student who has internalized the Ātman paradigm can decline any -an stem they encounter — including ones they have never seen before. That is the difference between memorizing Sanskrit and possessing Sanskrit grammar.

## The Bidirectional Progression

Sanskrit fluency runs in two directions.

**Forward (generation):**
```text
MEANING → SEMANTIC RELATIONSHIPS → ROOTS / STEMS → DERIVATION → INFLECTION → SANDHI → SANSKRIT
```

**Reverse (parsing):**
```text
SANSKRIT → SANDHI SPLIT → INFLECTION → STEM / ROOT → GRAMMATICAL RELATIONSHIP → MEANING
```

Reading Sanskrit is disassembly. Writing Sanskrit is assembly. This course trains both.

---

## The Deepest Arc

Across all 36 stages, the progression follows one line:

> **recognize → decompose → generate → compose → beautify → constrain → improvise**

Or in the language of the construction system:

> **identify bricks → understand connectors → build structures → build beautifully → build under constraints → build spontaneously**

# The 36 Stages

The course progresses like a construction game.

Every stage gives you a new set of components. Every later stage assumes you can freely use what came before.

At first, you build words.

Then sentences.

Then prayers.

Then poetry.

Eventually, you compose Sanskrit **under multiple simultaneous constraints**, culminating in the skills used in **avadhāna**.

---

## Phase I · Learn the Pieces

### Stages 1–7

| Stage               | Skill              | What you gain                                                 |
| ------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1 · Nāma**        | Vocabulary         | Collect your first semantic bricks — deities, pūjā objects, nature, body, sacred space |
| **2 · Varṇa-Vidyā** | Sound              | The physical material bricks are made of — every Sanskrit sound by articulation and structure |
| **3 · Sandhi**      | Joining            | The physics of connection — what happens at every joint       |
| **4 · Guṇa**        | Description        | Build rich descriptions: sundara, mahān, karuṇāmaya…          |
| **5 · Rūpa**        | Declension         | Noun connectors (sup) — how prātipadikas plug into sentences  |
| **6 · Kriyā**       | Verbs              | The verb control panel (tiṅ) — action, command, desire, tense |
| **7 · Kāraka**      | Sentence structure | Understand who does what to whom, where, how, and why         |

By the end of Phase I, you can construct sentences such as:

**ahaṃ bhaktyā mandire śivāya padmaṃ prāyaccham**

*I offered a lotus to Śiva with devotion in the temple.*

You are no longer repeating a sentence someone gave you. You know **why every word has the form it has**.

## Phase II · Learn to Build

### Stages 8–12

| Stage              | Skill            | What you gain                                      |
| ------------------ | ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **8 · Sambodhana** | Invocation       | Address the deity directly: *he devi!*             |
| **9 · Dhātu**      | Word generation  | Root families, upasargas, and the conversion kit (pratyaya) — intermediate consonant-stem families |
| **10 · Paryāya**   | Synonyms         | Develop multiple ways to express the same idea     |
| **11 · Samāsa**    | Compounds        | Fusing models into one — nīla + kaṇṭha → **nīlakaṇṭha** |
| **12 · Vākya**     | Free composition | Advanced prātipadikas (s-stems, root stems, pronouns, numerals); begin thinking and generating directly in Sanskrit |

At this point, Sanskrit stops being something you merely **decode**.

It becomes something you can **create with**.

You can invent epithets, describe deities, vary your vocabulary, and formulate original thoughts without translating every sentence from English first.

## Phase III · Build Devotional Language

### Stages 13–19

Now grammar becomes **sādhana**.

| Stage                   | Skill           | What you build                                                |
| ----------------------- | --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **13–14 · Stotra I–II** | Praise          | Compose original lines of devotional praise                   |
| **15 · Prārthanā**      | Prayer          | Write your own prayers and petitions                          |
| **16 · Pūjā-Vāk**       | Ritual Sanskrit | Parse and construct saṅkalpa, nyāsa, dhyāna and pūjā language |
| **17 · Kathā**          | Narrative       | Tell stories in Sanskrit                                      |
| **18 · Chandas I**      | Metre           | The building grid — hear and construct the rhythmic architecture of verse |

The purpose is not grammar for grammar's sake.

It is to move from:

**"I know what this mantra means."**

to:

**"I understand how this mantra was constructed."**

and eventually:

**"I can construct sacred Sanskrit myself."**

## Phase IV · Build Beautiful Things

### Stages 20–25

Correct Sanskrit is only the beginning.

Now we make it **beautiful**.

| Stage                      | Skill                | Challenge                                           |

## Phase V · Build Under Pressure

### Stages 26–36

Now the game changes.

You know the pieces. You know the grammar. You know the metre.

So we begin removing your freedom.

Compose a verse…

…but you must use these four words.

Now do it without the letter **ra**.

Now complete a verse whose final line has already been given.

Now remember three unfinished verses while someone interrupts you with questions.

This is the road toward **avadhāna**.

| Stage                       | Challenge           | Constraint                                             |
| --------------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| **26 · Samasyāpūraṇa**      | Verse completion    | Receive part of a verse and construct the rest         |
| **27 · Dattapadī**          | Forced vocabulary   | Incorporate words chosen by someone else               |
| **28 · Niṣiddhākṣarī**      | Forbidden letters   | Compose while avoiding selected sounds                 |
| **29 · Citra-Kāvya**        | Constraint stacking | Satisfy several poetic rules simultaneously            |
| **30–31 · Dhāraṇā I–II**    | Memory              | Hold multiple incomplete compositions at once          |
| **32 · Aprastuta-Prasaṅga** | Wit                 | Maintain composition while handling interruptions      |
| **33 · Multi-Devatā**       | Rapid switching     | Change subject and devotional register mid-composition |
| **34 · Aṣṭāvadhāna**        | Eightfold attention | Coordinate eight simultaneous challenges               |
| **35 · Sva-Avadhāna**       | Challenge design    | Construct your own constraint systems                  |
| **36 · Avadhāna-Sevā**      | Living mastery      | Use mastery as performance, offering and transmission  |

The final goal is not simply:

**"I know Sanskrit."**

It is:

**"Give me a meaning, a metre, a deity, a grammatical construction, a forbidden syllable, and four required words—and I can still create."**

# Sanskrit as Sādhana

This school approaches Sanskrit with a particular conviction:

**Sanskrit is not merely something to study. It is something to practice.**

You learn the accusative by praising the Devī.

You learn the dative by making offerings to Śiva.

You learn the vocative by calling to Kṛṣṇa.

You learn compounds by constructing divine names.

You learn metre by composing stotras.

You learn grammar until the distance between **language, thought, poetry, and prayer** begins to disappear.

We begin with:

**namaḥ**

We end with the ability to create an entire world from the same pieces.

**अध्ययनमेव साधना।
adhyayanam eva sādhanā.**

*Study itself is practice.*
