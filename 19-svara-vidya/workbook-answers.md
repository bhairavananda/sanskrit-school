# Svara-Vidyā — Workbook (Answers)

## Section A: The Three Svaras

**A1.**
1. **Udātta** — "raised." The high-pitched syllable — the peak of a word's tonal contour. In KYV notation: **unmarked** (no special sign).
2. **Anudātta** — "not raised." The low-pitched syllable — the valley or baseline. In KYV notation: **horizontal line below** the syllable (underline).
3. **Svarita** — "sounded" (compound pitch). A falling pitch that occurs immediately after an udātta syllable. In KYV notation: **vertical bar above** the syllable.

**A2.** The KYV system marks the *support structure,* not the peak. If you know where the anudātta (low) and svarita (falling) syllables are, the udātta is identified by elimination — it is the unmarked syllable between them. This is efficient: in a long word with many syllables, only one is udātta, so marking the (potentially many) anudātta syllables and the (always exactly one) svarita leaves the udātta unambiguously identified without a mark. The system treats the low pitch as the thing that needs flagging, since the high pitch is the exception that stands out by contrast.

**A3.**
1. The priest intended: "*indraśatro vardhasva*" — "O you whose enemy is Indra (i.e., O Indra's foe), grow!" — addressing Vṛtra with a vocative, meaning "you, Indra's enemy, may you grow."
2. The wrong svara shifted the compound accent, making it: "*indraśatruḥ vardhasva*" — "May Indra's enemy grow!" — a wish *about* Vṛtra rather than *addressed to* Vṛtra.
3. The mantra turned against its speaker — Vṛtra's own priest caused his patron's destruction rather than empowerment.
4. **Svara is not cosmetic — it determines the grammatical structure and therefore the meaning.** A change in pitch-accent changes whether a compound is a vocative address or a nominative statement, which changes the entire target and direction of the mantra.
5. Mispronouncing a varṇa (letter) gives you a wrong word or nonsense. Mispronouncing a svara gives you a *grammatically valid but differently-meaning* expression — which is more dangerous, because the mantra still "works," just at the wrong target.

## Section B: Svara Rules

**B1.**

1. **ag**nim → **U S** (udātta on "ag," svarita on "nim")
2. na**maḥ** → **A U** (anudātta on "na," udātta on "maḥ")
3. **so**māya → **U S A** (udātta on "so," svarita on "mā," anudātta on "ya")
4. ru**drā**ya → **A U S** (anudātta on "ru," udātta on "drā," svarita on "ya")
5. tri**yam**bakam → **A U S A** (anudātta on "tri," udātta on "yam," svarita on "ba," anudātta on "kam")
6. su**gan**dhim → **A U S** (anudātta on "su," udātta on "gan," svarita on "dhim")
7. **puṣ**ṭi → **U S** (udātta on "puṣ," svarita on "ṭi")
8. var**dha**nam → **A U S** (anudātta on "var," udātta on "dha," svarita on "nam")
9. u**rvā**rukam → **A U S A** (anudātta on "u," udātta on "rvā," svarita on "ru," anudātta on "kam") — as heard in standard Rudram recitation
10. mṛ**tyor** → **A U** (anudātta on "mṛ," udātta on "tyor")

**B2.**
1. The first is **anudātta** (low — before the rise) and the third is **svarita** (falling — after the peak).
2. It becomes **svarita** — the pitch falls after the udātta peak.
3. It **loses its udātta** — the finite verb becomes fully anudātta (accentless) in a main clause. This is why Vedic prose (brāhmaṇa) sounds "flatter" than Vedic hymns (mantra).
4. The **first member** typically loses its independent accent. The compound carries the accent of the second member (with exceptions).
5. A **kampa-svarita** (trembling/wavering pitch) may occur at the boundary, or one of the udāttas may be suppressed according to Prātiśākhya rules. The exact resolution depends on the syntactic relationship and is specified by the Prātiśākhya sūtras.

**B3.** The Sāmaveda takes the three Vedic svaras and maps them onto a seven-note musical scale:

- Udātta → the high note(s)
- Anudātta → the low note(s)
- Svarita → the transitional/falling note(s)

The Sāmaveda recitation system (sāmagāna) assigns specific numerical values (1-7) to pitches, creating actual melodies from what in the Ṛgveda and Yajurveda is a three-level accent system. This is why Indian musicological tradition considers the Sāmaveda the origin of saṅgīta — it is the point where Vedic tonal recitation becomes music. The seven svaras of Indian music (ṣaḍja, ṛṣabha, gāndhāra, madhyama, pañcama, dhaivata, niṣāda) are an elaboration of the three Vedic svaras. Recitation became melody became rāga.

## Section C: Saṃhitā vs Pada

**C1.** The sandhi of mā + amṛtāt:

mā + amṛtāt → **māmṛtāt** (the ā of mā combines with the a of amṛtāt → māmṛtāt by dīrgha-sandhi: ā + a → ā)

Complete saṃhitā:
```
oṃ tryambakaṃ yajāmahe sugandhiṃ puṣṭivardhanam |
urvārukamiva bandhanānmṛtyormukṣīya māmṛtāt |
```

(Note: bandhanāt + mṛtyoḥ → bandhanānmṛtyoḥ by visarga-sandhi; mṛtyoḥ + mukṣīya → mṛtyormukṣīya by visarga-sandhi; mā + amṛtāt → māmṛtāt.)

**C2.**
1. **Saṃhitā preserves** the continuous sound-stream as it is actually recited — including the svara contour that operates across word-boundaries and the specific sandhi-generated sounds that exist only in combination.
2. **Pada reveals** the individual words and their independent forms — showing the original visarga, the pre-sandhi vowels, the word-boundaries that sandhi obscures.
3. **Saṃhitā** is used in ritual recitation — it is the performed, living form of the mantra.
4. **Pada** is used for grammatical analysis — it shows what words are present, their cases, their stems.
5. The relationship IS sandhi — saṃhitā is the text *with* sandhi applied; pada is the text *before* sandhi. The pada-pāṭha is the analytical key to the saṃhitā-pāṭha. Understanding both means understanding sandhi as a reversible process: given pada, you can construct saṃhitā; given saṃhitā, you can reconstruct pada.

**C3.** In saṃhitā-pāṭha, each word appears once in a continuous stream. If word B is corrupted to B', only one instance exists, and if the change is subtle, it may not be noticed.

In krama-pāṭha, word B appears in TWO pairs: AB and BC. If B is corrupted to B':
- Pair 1 becomes AB' — something sounds wrong because A-B' is an unfamiliar combination
- Pair 2 becomes B'C — again unfamiliar
- The correct form B is remembered from the two overlapping contexts

The redundancy means every word is *cross-checked* against its neighbors in both directions. A corruption that might slip past in a linear reading is caught by the overlap. This redundancy increases dramatically in jaṭā (6× per word) and ghana (10×+ per word).

## Section D: The Vikṛtis

**D1.** Krama-pāṭha for: namaḥ | śivāya | ca | mahādevāya | ca

```
Step 1: namaḥ śivāya
Step 2: śivāya ca
Step 3: ca mahādevāya
Step 4: mahādevāya ca
Step 5: ca iti ca
```

**D2.** Jaṭā-pāṭha (first two pairs):

```
Pair 1 (namaḥ, śivāya):
Forward:  namaḥ śivāya
Backward: śivāya namaḥ
Forward:  namaḥ śivāya

Pair 2 (śivāya, ca):
Forward:  śivāya ca
Backward: ca śivāya
Forward:  śivāya ca
```

**D3.** Ghana-pāṭha (first group: namaḥ, śivāya, ca):

```
AB:    namaḥ śivāya
BA:    śivāya namaḥ
ABC:   namaḥ śivāya ca
CBA:   ca śivāya namaḥ
ABC:   namaḥ śivāya ca
```

**D4.**
1. **Preservation:** Each word appears in multiple overlapping contexts (forward and backward). If any word is corrupted, the error breaks the pattern in several places simultaneously. In ghana-pāṭha, a single word appears 10+ times in different configurations — accidentally changing one instance while all others remain correct is virtually impossible. This interlocking redundancy has preserved Vedic texts with near-perfect fidelity for over 3,000 years, predating writing.

2. **Sonic architecture:** When words are woven forward and backward, the sound-patterns overlap and interfere constructively. Linear recitation produces a one-dimensional stream. Jaṭā produces a two-dimensional weave (forward + backward). Ghana produces a three-dimensional structure (forward + backward + triple-compound). The result is overtone patterns and resonance effects that do not exist in linear recitation — the woven sound fills acoustic space differently. Chamaka-ghana, for instance, produces a quality of sustained resonance that simple Chamakam recitation does not.

3. **Dhāraṇā training:** To recite ghana-pāṭha, the reciter must simultaneously hold: (a) the current position in the source text, (b) the forward sequence, (c) the backward sequence, (d) the triple-compound sequence, and (e) where to shift next. This is *divided attention on multiple simultaneous threads* — exactly the skill required for avadhāna, where you hold eight challenges at once, cycling between them while remembering the state of each. The vikṛtis are, in effect, avadhāna training built into the recitation tradition. A competent ghana-pāṭhī already has the core dhāraṇā skill that stages 27-31 of this syllabus develop.

## Section E: Two Dimensions of the Syllable

**E1.**
1. **Guru** — because ā is a long vowel. Long vowels always make a syllable guru (heavy / 2 mātrā).
2. **Svarita** — because "so" (the preceding syllable) is udātta, and the syllable immediately after udātta becomes svarita (falling pitch).
3. Independence means: a syllable's weight (laghu/guru) is determined by vowel-length and consonant-clustering, while its pitch (udātta/anudātta/svarita) is determined by word-accent rules. The two systems use different criteria and can produce any combination:
   - **Laghu + udātta:** The syllable "a" in "agním" — short vowel (laghu), but it carries the udātta (high pitch).
   - **Guru + anudātta:** The syllable "mā" in a word where it is not near the udātta — long vowel (guru), but low pitch (anudātta).

**E2.**
- **Lost:** The pitch-accent system — classical Sanskrit no longer distinguishes udātta/anudātta/svarita. Words that were distinguished by accent alone became homophones. The tonal dimension of the language collapsed.
- **Retained:** The syllable-weight system (laghu/guru) — classical Sanskrit poetry is entirely built on this. Every śloka, every śārdūlavikrīḍita, every mandākrāntā is defined by patterns of light and heavy syllables. Chandas survived; svara did not.
- **Consequence:** Classical Sanskrit poetry controls *rhythm* (through weight-patterns) but not *pitch* (which is free). Vedic recitation controls both. This is why Vedic recitation has a distinctive melodic quality that classical śloka recitation does not — the pitch is prescribed, not improvised.

**E3.**
- **Weight-pattern** tells you the *meter* — which chandas the verse is in, whether the syllable-pattern matches anuṣṭubh or gāyatrī or triṣṭubh. It answers: "what is the rhythmic structure?"
- **Pitch-pattern** tells you the *word-identity and grammatical structure* — where each word peaks, how compounds are accented, whether a verb has lost its accent. It answers: "what do the words mean and how do they relate?"

Weight is about the *body* of the verse (its rhythmic skeleton). Pitch is about the *meaning* of the verse (its semantic contour). A metrical analysis tells you how to *scan* the verse. A svara analysis tells you how to *understand* it.

## Section F: Synthesis

**F1.**

1. **Varṇa** — controls the *identity* of the sound (which phoneme). Learned in Varṇa-Vidyā (after Stage 1). Getting it wrong: you produce a different word entirely or nonsense. A mispronounced letter is a different letter.

2. **Svara** — controls the *pitch* of the syllable (high/low/falling). Learned in this stage (Svara-Vidyā, after Chandas I). Getting it wrong: the Indraśatru effect — the mantra is grammatically valid but semantically redirected. The power goes to the wrong target.

3. **Mātrā** — controls the *duration* of the syllable (short/long/extended). Learned in Chandas I. Getting it wrong: the meter breaks — the verse no longer fits its chandas, which in Vedic understanding means it loses the breath-pattern that makes it effective. A gāyatrī with wrong mātrā is no longer gāyatrī.

**F2.** The KYV's interleaving of mantra and brāhmaṇa creates a distinctive svara landscape:

- **Mantra sections** (hymns, invocations) use full accent — every word carries its udātta, and the recitation has a strong tonal contour with clear peaks and valleys.
- **Brāhmaṇa sections** (explanatory prose) have more main-clause finite verbs, and these verbs lose their accent. The result is "flatter" — more anudātta, fewer peaks.

In a text like the Taittirīya Saṃhitā, the reciter shifts between these two tonal registers as they move from mantra to brāhmaṇa and back. The svara contour itself signals which mode you are in — the ear can distinguish mantra from brāhmaṇa by the density of udātta peaks. This tonal switching is unique to the KYV (the Śukla Yajurveda separates mantra and brāhmaṇa into different texts; the KYV weaves them). Learning to hear this switch — from the peaked, melodic mantra-voice to the flatter brāhmaṇa-voice and back — is a distinctive skill of KYV recitation.
