# Stage 20: The One Who...

## Pattern Name: Relative-Correlative ("yaḥ...saḥ")

---

## The Pattern

**yaḥ X karoti saḥ Y bhavati** — "He who does X becomes Y"
**yat pustakam tvam paṭhasi tat mama asti** — "The book which you read is mine"

---

## How It Works

Sanskrit doesn't use "who" or "which" the way English does. Instead, it uses a two-part system: a relative clause starting with *yaḥ* (who/which) and a correlative clause starting with *saḥ* (he/that). They come in matched pairs and must agree in gender and number. Think of it as a call-and-response: *yaḥ* calls out the person or thing, and *saḥ* answers by pointing to them.

The forms shift with gender: *yaḥ...saḥ* (masculine), *yā...sā* (feminine), *yat...tat* (neuter). For plural: *ye...te* (masc.), *yāḥ...tāḥ* (fem.), *yāni...tāni* (neut.). The relative clause can come first or second -- Sanskrit is flexible. These constructions show up everywhere in proverbs and everyday speech, so once you have this lego piece, you can build surprisingly complex thoughts.

## Example Phrases

1. **यः पठति सः जानाति।**
   *yaḥ paṭhati saḥ jānāti.*
   He who studies, knows.

2. **या स्त्री अत्र गायति सा मम भगिनी अस्ति।**
   *yā strī atra gāyati sā mama bhaginī asti.*
   The woman who sings here is my sister.

3. **यत् फलं त्वम् इच्छसि तत् गृहाण।**
   *yat phalaṃ tvam icchasi tat gṛhāṇa.*
   Take the fruit which you want.

4. **ये जनाः सत्यं वदन्ति ते सुखिनः भवन्ति।**
   *ye janāḥ satyaṃ vadanti te sukhinaḥ bhavanti.*
   Those people who speak truth become happy.

5. **यत् जलं त्वं पिबसि तत् शुद्धम् अस्ति।**
   *yat jalaṃ tvaṃ pibasi tat śuddham asti.*
   The water which you drink is clean.

6. **यः बालकः क्रीडति सः मम पुत्रः अस्ति।**
   *yaḥ bālakaḥ krīḍati saḥ mama putraḥ asti.*
   The boy who is playing is my son.

7. **यस्मिन् ग्रामे अहं वसामि तत् ग्रामं सुन्दरम् अस्ति।**
   *yasmin grāme ahaṃ vasāmi tat grāmaṃ sundaram asti.*
   The village in which I live is beautiful.

8. **या भाषा त्वं वदसि सा मधुरा अस्ति।**
   *yā bhāṣā tvaṃ vadasi sā madhurā asti.*
   The language which you speak is sweet.

9. **यत् कार्यं त्वं करोषि तत् शोभनम् अस्ति।**
   *yat kāryaṃ tvaṃ karoṣi tat śobhanam asti.*
   The work which you do is excellent.

10. **यः गुरुं सेवते सः विद्यां लभते।**
    *yaḥ guruṃ sevate saḥ vidyāṃ labhate.*
    He who serves the teacher obtains knowledge.

## Mini-Dialogues

**Dialogue 1: Who is that?**

-- सः बालकः कः अस्ति?
   *saḥ bālakaḥ kaḥ asti?*
   Who is that boy?

-- यः बालकः नीलं वस्त्रं धारयति सः मम मित्रम् अस्ति।
   *yaḥ bālakaḥ nīlaṃ vastraṃ dhārayati saḥ mama mitram asti.*
   The boy who is wearing blue clothes is my friend.

-- आम्, यः हसति सः एव?
   *ām, yaḥ hasati saḥ eva?*
   Yes, the one who is laughing?

-- आम्, सः एव।
   *ām, saḥ eva.*
   Yes, that's him.

**Dialogue 2: A proverb exchange**

-- एकं सूक्तिं वद।
   *ekaṃ sūktiṃ vada.*
   Tell me a proverb.

-- यः श्रमं करोति सः फलं लभते।
   *yaḥ śramaṃ karoti saḥ phalaṃ labhate.*
   He who works hard obtains the fruit.

-- सुन्दरम्! अपरम् अपि — या विद्या दानेन वर्धते सा एव सत्या विद्या।
   *sundaram! aparam api — yā vidyā dānena vardhate sā eva satyā vidyā.*
   Beautiful! Another one too — the knowledge which grows by giving is the true knowledge.

## Variations

1. **Locative relative (yasmin...tasmin):**
   *yasmin nagare saḥ vasati tasmin nagare mandiraṃ asti* — "In the city where he lives, there is a temple."

2. **With instrumental (yena...tena):**
   *yena mārgena tvaṃ gacchasi tena mārgena aham api gacchāmi* — "I also go by the path by which you go."

3. **Compact form (dropping saḥ when obvious):**
   *yaḥ paṭhati jānāti* — "He who reads, knows." (The *saḥ* is implied.)

---

## Common Mistakes

1. **Gender mismatch:** If the noun is feminine (*nadī*), you need *yā...sā*, not *yaḥ...saḥ*. The relative and correlative must match the gender of the noun they refer to, not the gender of some other noun in the sentence.

2. **Forgetting the correlative:** In English we say "the man who came is my friend" -- one clause. In Sanskrit you need the echo: *yaḥ naraḥ āgataḥ **saḥ** mama mitraṃ asti.* Dropping *saḥ* can work in poetry but sounds incomplete in conversation.
