# Stage 10: Vākya — Free Composition

## Goal
Compose freely in Sanskrit — describe daily life, thoughts, and experiences. Begin thinking directly in Sanskrit without translating from English.

## The Shift: From Components to Complete Scenes

Stages 1–9 gave you the full construction system — dhātus (engines), upasargas (modifier attachments), pratyayas (conversion kits), prātipadikas (finished subassemblies), sup connectors, tiṅ control panels, sandhi (physics of the snap), and samāsas (fused subassemblies). Now you combine them fluidly into vākyas — complete scenes. This stage is about **automaticity** — moving from conscious rule-application to natural expression.

## Sentence Patterns for Daily Life

### Pattern 1: Simple Statement (Subject + Verb)
> ahaṃ paṭhāmi — I study
> sūryaḥ udeti — the sun rises
> vṛṣṭiḥ bhavati — rain happens

### Pattern 2: Transitive (Subject + Object + Verb)
> ahaṃ pustakam paṭhāmi — I read a book
> mātā bhojanaṃ pacati — mother cooks food
> bālaḥ jalami pibati — the child drinks water

### Pattern 3: Full Sentence (S + Instrument + Location + Object + Verb)
> ahaṃ prātaḥ gṛhe bhojanaṃ karomi — I eat food at home in the morning

### Pattern 4: Compound Sentence (X ca Y ca)
> ahaṃ paṭhāmi likhāmi ca — I study and write
> sūryaḥ udeti candraḥ ca astam gacchati — the sun rises and the moon sets

### Pattern 5: Relative Clause (yaḥ... saḥ...)
> yaḥ bhajati saḥ mucyate — he who worships is liberated
> yā devī sarva-bhūteṣu... — she who is the goddess in all beings...

## Building Longer Passages

### Describing a Place
> mama gṛham sundaram asti. tatra ekaṃ mandiram asti. mandire śivasya mūrtiḥ asti. pratidinṃ ahaṃ tatra pūjāṃ karomi. mandiraṃ śāntam asti. tatra ahaṃ dhyānaṃ karomi.

### Describing a Person
> mama guruḥ mahān asti. saḥ śāntaḥ asti. saḥ jñānī asti. saḥ pradinaṃ vedān paṭhati. saḥ śiṣyān pāṭhayati. ahaṃ tasya śiṣyaḥ asmi.

### Narrating an Event
> hyaḥ ahaṃ mandiraṃ gacchāmi (agaccham). tatra mahāpūjā āsīt. bahavaḥ bhaktāḥ āsan. sarve militvā stotram agāyan. atiśobhanam āsīt.

## Thinking in Sanskrit — Tips

1. **Start with objects around you** — name them in Sanskrit
2. **Narrate your actions** as you do them: uttiṣṭhāmi, gacchāmi...
3. **Use simple sentences first**, then add cases and qualifiers
4. **Don't translate from English** — think in Subject-Object-Verb order
5. **Talk to your deity** daily in Sanskrit — even one sentence

## Milestone Check

Can you speak for 2 minutes in simple Sanskrit about your day, your surroundings, or your devotional practice — without pausing to translate from English?

## Advanced Baseplates: The Remaining Stem Families

By now you have 11 baseplates (7 vowel + 4 consonant). These cover most nouns in devotional and philosophical Sanskrit.

But real texts — especially Vedic passages, ritual manuals, and classical kāvya — contain additional stem families. Here are the remaining ones you need for complete coverage.

### 1. Neuter -as Stems: manas, tapas, yaśas, tejas

These are **extremely common** in philosophical Sanskrit. Nearly every abstract quality word is an -as stem: manas (mind), tapas (austerity), yaśas (glory), tejas (brilliance), śiras (head), vacas (speech, archaic), rajas (dust/passion), tamas (darkness/inertia), sattvas... the list is vast.

**Paradigm for manas (mind) — neuter:**

| Case | Singular | Dual | Plural |
|------|----------|------|--------|
| 1. Nominative | manaḥ | manasī | manāṃsi |
| 2. Accusative | manaḥ | manasī | manāṃsi |
| 3. Instrumental | manasā | manobhyām | manobhiḥ |
| 4. Dative | manase | manobhyām | manobhyaḥ |
| 5. Ablative | manasaḥ | manobhyām | manobhyaḥ |
| 6. Genitive | manasaḥ | manasoḥ | manasām |
| 7. Locative | manasi | manasoḥ | manaḥsu |
| 8. Vocative | manaḥ | manasī | manāṃsi |

Key patterns to internalize:
- Nom/Acc singular ends in **-aḥ** (visarga) — manaḥ, tapaḥ, yaśaḥ
- Instrumental singular: **-asā** — manasā, tapasā
- Locative singular: **-asi** — manasi, tapasi
- Before bh- suffixes, -as becomes -o- (sandhi): manobhiḥ, manobhyām

**Sacred context:** The Gītā's phrase *manasā vacasā karmaṇā* ("by mind, by speech, by action") uses manas in the instrumental. Every Upaniṣadic discussion of "mind" uses manas. The three guṇas — sattva, rajas, tamas — are all -as stems.

### 2. Neuter -is Stems: havis, jyotis, cakṣis

Less common than -as stems but important in ritual vocabulary. The pattern closely mirrors -as stems with predictable vowel changes.

**Key forms for havis (oblation) — neuter:**

| Case | Singular | Dual | Plural |
|------|----------|------|--------|
| 1. Nominative | haviḥ | haviṣī | havīṃṣi |
| 2. Accusative | haviḥ | haviṣī | havīṃṣi |
| 3. Instrumental | haviṣā | havirbhyām | havirbhiḥ |
| 4. Dative | haviṣe | havirbhyām | havirbhyaḥ |
| 5. Ablative | haviṣaḥ | havirbhyām | havirbhyaḥ |
| 6. Genitive | haviṣaḥ | haviṣoḥ | haviṣām |
| 7. Locative | haviṣi | haviṣoḥ | haviḥṣu |
| 8. Vocative | haviḥ | haviṣī | havīṃṣi |

Notice: the -s of the stem becomes **-ṣ** before vowels and certain consonants (retroflexion after i). This is a regular sandhi pattern.

**Sacred context:** havis is THE ritual offering word — "agnaū haviḥ juhoti" (one offers the oblation into fire). jyotis (light) appears throughout the Upaniṣads: "tamaso mā jyotirgamaya" — "lead me from darkness to light."

### 3. Neuter -us Stems: cakṣus, āyus, dhanus

Important body and attribute vocabulary. The pattern again mirrors -as/-is with its own vowel.

**Key forms for cakṣus (eye) — neuter:**

| Case | Singular | Dual | Plural |
|------|----------|------|--------|
| 1. Nominative | cakṣuḥ | cakṣuṣī | cakṣūṃṣi |
| 2. Accusative | cakṣuḥ | cakṣuṣī | cakṣūṃṣi |
| 3. Instrumental | cakṣuṣā | cakṣurbhyām | cakṣurbhiḥ |
| 4. Dative | cakṣuṣe | cakṣurbhyām | cakṣurbhyaḥ |
| 5. Ablative | cakṣuṣaḥ | cakṣurbhyām | cakṣurbhyaḥ |
| 6. Genitive | cakṣuṣaḥ | cakṣuṣoḥ | cakṣuṣām |
| 7. Locative | cakṣuṣi | cakṣuṣoḥ | cakṣuḥṣu |
| 8. Vocative | cakṣuḥ | cakṣuṣī | cakṣūṃṣi |

The -s becomes -ṣ after u, just as it became -ṣ after i in havis.

**Sacred context:** āyus (life, lifespan) is central to Vedic prayer — "śataṃ jīvema śaradaḥ" is paired with wishes for long āyus. dhanus (bow) is inseparable from Rāma (the bearer of the great bow) and Śiva (the holder of Pināka).

### 4. Root Consonant Stems: vāc, diś, marut

These are monosyllabic (or near-monosyllabic) stems ending in a consonant with no derivational suffix — the "raw bricks" of the noun system. The stem IS the root in many cases.

**Paradigm for vāc (speech) — feminine:**

| Case | Singular | Dual | Plural |
|------|----------|------|--------|
| 1. Nominative | vāk | vācau | vācaḥ |
| 2. Accusative | vācam | vācau | vācaḥ |
| 3. Instrumental | vācā | vāgbhyām | vāgbhiḥ |
| 4. Dative | vāce | vāgbhyām | vāgbhyaḥ |
| 5. Ablative | vācaḥ | vāgbhyām | vāgbhyaḥ |
| 6. Genitive | vācaḥ | vācoḥ | vācām |
| 7. Locative | vāci | vācoḥ | vākṣu |
| 8. Vocative | vāk | vācau | vācaḥ |

Key observations:
- The nominative singular shows the bare stem with final consonant adjustment: vāc → vāk (the palatal c becomes velar k at word-end)
- Before bh- endings, c becomes g: vāgbhiḥ
- The strong/weak stem distinction is minimal — the stem stays vāc- throughout, with an inserted -a- before consonant endings

Other root stems: diś (direction, f.) — "daśa diśaḥ" (the ten directions); marut (storm-god, m.) — the Maruts of the Vedas; samit (sacrificial stick, f.); ap (water, f., plural only — apaḥ).

### 5. Pronominal Declension

Pronouns — tad (that), idam (this), kim (what?), sarva (all), etad (this here) — follow their own special patterns. You already met the personal pronouns (aham, tvam) in Stage 5 and the demonstrative forms (saḥ, sā, tat) in daily use.

The key difference: pronominal stems use -smin (loc sg) and -smai (dat sg) instead of the regular noun endings -e and -āya. The adjective sarva (all) also follows pronominal declension.

### 6. Numerals

Number words have special declensions:
- **eka** (one): declines like a pronominal adjective (singular only)
- **dvi** (two): dual only — dvau, dvābhyām, dvayoḥ
- **tri** (three): trayaḥ (m), tisraḥ (f), trīṇi (n) — plural only
- **catur** (four): catvāraḥ (m), catasraḥ (f), catvāri (n) — plural only
- **pañcan** onwards: decline as plural nouns

Full paradigms are not given here — the point is awareness. When you encounter a numeral in a text, recognize it belongs to its own declension family.

> **With these additions, you possess the complete set of Sanskrit baseplates. Any noun you encounter in any text — Vedic, classical, devotional, philosophical — falls into one of these families. The principle remains: identify the stem type, recall the model, generate the form.**
